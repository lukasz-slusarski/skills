# Borderline examples

Rulings under AI Act Article 50(4) and the Commission guidelines C(2026) 5054 (point numbers in brackets), as of 8.10.2026. Each example assumes professional activity (a company, agency, a creator earning from a profile). Assess platform rules separately in [platforms.md](platforms.md).

## Anchors from the Commission guidelines

Examples given directly by the Commission. When a case is similar to them, rule the same way.

| Deepfake, label | Not a deepfake |
|---|---|
| a generated likeness of a well-known influencer in an ad | mice arguing in a human voice about cheese in an ad |
| a realistic CEO avatar congratulating employees | a cartoon generated from a historical photo |
| a voice clone of podcast hosts talking about the news | a real car in an ad against an AI background, without misleading about the car |
| a generated product image prettier than reality | AI voices of fictional characters in an audiobook, game, animation |
| a manipulated photo of real footballers in front of a "stadium" | a presenter in a real recording with an AI animation in the background |
| a politician in a generated speech to a crowd | a denoised broadcast with no change of words |

## Image and video with your own face

**1. Own face in a clearly stylized graphic** (cartoon, watercolor, Pixar style, 3D figurine) for a LinkedIn post.
Law: no obligation. Nobody will take an illustration for a real photo (fourth condition), just like the cartoon from a historical photo in the guidelines.
The same result for a stylized face of another person (e.g. a client's CEO), but then their consent to likeness is needed.
Label: optionally "Graphic: AI".

**2. Own face in a photorealistic graphic** (e.g. "me on a conference stage", "me in an office in New York") where you have not been.
Law: **label**. An existing person, a scene that did not happen, the recipient may take it for a real photo. Your own consent does not release you: the provision protects the recipient from being misled, not the person in the photo. A caption like "I'll be here one day" is not a label; the label must say plainly that it is AI.
Platforms: check [platforms.md](platforms.md).

**3. A photorealistic business photo generated from a selfie** ("AI headshot"), looks like an ordinary photo from a session.
Law: UNCERTAIN, recommendation: **label** discreetly ("Photo generated by AI"). For: an existing person, looks like a photo nobody took. Against: it shows how you really look, it does not describe an event. This is not a minor edit under point 116, because the image was created from scratch.

**4. Retouching a photo from a session** (smoothing skin, removing a pimple, correcting light, cutting out the background to a plain one, removing passers-by).
Law: no obligation. Cosmetic corrections and technical adjustments do not make a deepfake (116).
Boundary: changing the figure, adding people, objects or a place that changes the message (e.g. "results" in an ad for a slimming supplement) is manipulation: **label**, and in advertising expect also a charge of misleading.

## Editing and sound

**5. AI editing of your own recording**: automatic cutting of pauses, captions, denoising, cropping, color correction.
Law: no obligation. The recording is authentic, AI performs technical editing (116).

**6. Own recording with content changed by AI**: lip-sync to a new text, sentences dubbed with a cloned voice, the background replaced with a place you have not been, "corrected" eye contact.
Law: a change of words, voice or place: **label** (the recipient will take it that it was so). Eye contact correction alone: no obligation, because it does not change the message; in advertising UNCERTAIN.

**7. Voice clone** (own or a voice-over artist's) reading a text in a podcast, ad, video.
Own voice: **label**. The listener will think it is you speaking, while the recording is synthetic (cf. the voice clone of podcast hosts in the guidelines). A spoken notice at the start, and for a longer recording reminders during it (point 141).
Another person's voice: **label**, and that person's consent is needed (voice is a personal right). Do not publish without consent.
An AI voice of a fictional narrator or character that does not pretend to be a specific person: no deepfake under the guidelines; for clarity you may add "Narrator: AI voice".

**8. AI avatar** (video with a photorealistic digital presenter).
Law: **label**, also when the avatar is an invented person. "Persons" include realistic avatars and AI personas, and it is enough that the person could exist (113). A digital twin of a real person all the more. A clearly cartoonish, unrealistic avatar: no obligation.

## Products and places

**9. A product in an AI setting.**
A real product photo with a background, interior or landscape generated for aesthetic purposes: no obligation, as long as the scene does not mislead about the product (the car anchor, point 116).
A product generated by AI itself or "improved" so that it looks better than it really is: **label** (the product image anchor); additionally a risk of misleading the consumer.
A generated "photo from a project", "our salon", "a satisfied customer": **label** (a place or person that could exist, presented as real).

**10. A conceptual illustration** (abstraction, icons, a blog graphic, "a robot at a laptop").
Law: no obligation. It does not realistically depict people, places or events, or it is obviously stylized. A photorealistic scene with people, e.g. "a team at a desk", is however subject to the conditions from example 8.

## Text

**11. Improving the style of a text written by a human** (proofreading, shortening, "make it sound natural", translation).
Law: no obligation. This is editing, not generating; for a text on public matters, AI translation after human verification fits within the editorial exception (guidelines, translation example).

**12. An expert LinkedIn post written by AI**, an industry topic (e.g. "5 SEO mistakes"), the author read it, checked it and publishes under their own name.
Law: no obligation. A narrow industry topic is usually not a matter of public interest, and if it were, the author checked the facts and answers for the text under their own name. Where the post concerns health, law, public finances or politics, and the author only read it without fact-checking: **label**.

**13. An article about changes in law, health, elections** generated by AI and published automatically on a blog.
Law: **label** (matters of public interest, no verification). The obligation falls away only when a person with subject-matter knowledge checks the facts (proofreading alone is not enough, 134-135), AI does not change the text after that verification (136), and the details of the responsible person or company are publicly available, e.g. in the footer (138).

**14. A product description or advertising text generated by AI.**
Law: no obligation (131). Exception: the description contains claims about health, consumer safety or sustainability ("boosts immunity", "climate neutral"); then such a claim alone is enough to assess the description as a text on public matters, with the editorial exception. A label on every such description. The rules on health claims are a separate topic, outside this skill.

**15. A chatbot on a client's site** answering users.
This is not a question about labeling a publication but about Article 50(1): the user is to know they are talking to an AI. A message "You are talking to an AI assistant" at the start of the conversation.

## Satire and creative work

**16. A satirical clip with an AI-generated politician.**
Law: a deepfake, but an **evidently satirical** work: the obligation is softened, disclosure without spoiling reception (description, title card, captions). If the satire is not obvious (a realistic statement, a serious tone), treat it as an ordinary deepfake. On YouTube and TikTok the description is often collapsed: add a title card or an on-screen caption. TikTok bans, even with a label, showing a public figure taking a position they did not take ([platforms.md](platforms.md)). Watch out also for electoral law and personal rights.

**17. A music video, a short fiction film with generated scenes.**
Law: a creative work, disclosure in the credits or description (Commitment 3 of the Code).

**18. A witty ad with a realistic generated "customer".**
Law: **label** in the ordinary way. Commercial content does not benefit from the regime for works (122), and humor does not change the character of an ad.
