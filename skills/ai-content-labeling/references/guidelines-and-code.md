# Commission guidelines and the Code of Practice

Two documents that explain Article 50. Neither is law: the guidelines are a non-binding Commission interpretation, the Code is a voluntary commitment of its signatories. In practice supervisory authorities and courts will refer to them. Read this file when a case is not settled directly in the SKILL.md decision tree.

## Who is a deployer

- (19) Professional activity is any from which a natural person regularly derives economic benefit or conducts a business, commercial, professional or freelance activity. A creator earning from a profile, a freelancer, a company: professional.
- (19) Persons acting on behalf of or under the control of the deployer (an employee, a subcontractor) fall within its obligations.
- An agency that uses AI to produce an ad is a deployer. The client commissioning the ad does not become one if it does not decide whether and how the agency uses AI. Practical conclusion: the labeling obligation lies with the agency, even when the client publishes; worth settling in the contract.

## Deepfake: four conditions together (113)

1. **Resemblance**: a high degree of resemblance to the original, recognizable features; it does not have to be identical.
2. **Existing**: it is enough that the depicted entity **exists, can exist or could have existed**. A fictional but realistic person, place or scene meets this condition. Outside the scope only things contrary to the laws of nature (flying people, dragons, elephants driving cars).
3. **Persons, objects, places, entities, events**: "persons" include digital replicas of real people, **realistic avatars and personas generated by AI** and personal characteristics (likeness, voice, behavior, performance). "Events" include the depiction of professional or consumer services.
4. **Falsely authentic or truthful**: an objective assessment, intent to mislead is not needed (114). The whole counts: degree of resemblance, message, publication context and recipients' expectations. **Photorealism alone does not settle it**, but it makes it more likely (114). You assess by the real audience, not the "average" recipient: if children or older people may see it, what counts is whether they can be deceived (115).

### Minor edit (116)

Does not make content a deepfake: removing passers-by from the background, correcting light, sound parameters, colors, denoising, compression, accessibility improvements, **cosmetic corrections**. In advertising: color correction, extending the background, replacing the background for purely aesthetic purposes, arranging existing products, upscaling. It depends on context, however: heavy editing of the background of a journalistic photo may already mislead.

### Examples from the guidelines

Deepfake:
- a manipulated image of two real footballers in front of a building resembling a stadium;
- a voice clone of the hosts of a newspaper's podcast talking about the news;
- a generated video of a person resembling a politician addressing a crowd;
- **a generated likeness of a well-known influencer in an ad**;
- **a realistic avatar of a company CEO congratulating employees on results**;
- a generated image of a product in an ad or on packaging that may mislead about its appearance, features or use (prettier, of better quality than in reality).

Not a deepfake:
- a sphinx flying over the Eiffel Tower;
- mice arguing in a human voice about cheese in a cheese producer's ad;
- AI voices of fictional characters (audiobooks, games, animation), where there is no misleading about the narrator's identity;
- a technically improved broadcast (normalization, denoising) without changing the spoken words;
- **a cartoon generated from an existing photo of a historical event**;
- a presenter in a real recording with an AI animation in the background (receding glaciers);
- fictional environments in games;
- **a real product (e.g. a car) in an ad against an AI-generated background**, if the ad does not mislead about the product.

### Artistic, creative, satirical, fictional works (119-124)

- The categories are read narrowly (122): content of unclear character does not benefit from the lighter regime. Content that is **purely informational or commercial** does not either.
- Creative = requiring creative choices; content motivated mainly functionally or technically is not creative (120). Satire = criticism of society, politics, business or public figures by means of humor.
- An ad may be creative in specific cases, but where content combines informational and creative features, the informational character prevails.
- Not a work: a realistic influencer testing a sponsored product and focused on its functions; a teleshopping-style video with synthetic people.
- The lighter regime is still disclosure, only in a way that does not hamper reception (123).

## Text on matters of public interest (131)

Three conditions together:
1. **Published**: available to an undefined, fairly large number of unconnected readers. Correspondence, a small closed group, an intranet are not published.
2. **Informs**: conveys knowledge, opinions or facts. Short texts that do not substantially do this do not inform.
3. **Matters of public interest**: relevant to society (locally, nationally, in the EU) and deserving debate: politics and democratic processes, administration, the justice system, fundamental rights, public security, **public health, environmental protection, consumer safety**, as well as any economic, financial, political, scientific or cultural development that may be the subject of public debate.

Require labeling (unless there is human verification): an AI summary of an article about a city council decision; AI-altered fragments of a lifestyle article about the effect of diets on an illness; reports of a listed company to investors; a weather warning of an institute on social media.

Out of scope: fantasy novels; **advertising text and product descriptions altered by AI, except claims concerning e.g. health, consumer safety or sustainability**; a news summary visible only to the person who asked; an adviser's advice to a client.

### Exception: human verification and editorial responsibility (133-140)

Both conditions together:
- **Human review or editorial control** (134): a deliberate substantive examination of the content by a person with professional knowledge and judgment; **fact-checking is the minimum**. Editorial control = the power to approve, change or reject the content.
- (135) Not enough: checking spelling or grammar, the mere existence of an editorial policy, automated review, cursory approval.
- (136) A substantive intervention by AI **after** verification voids the exception.
- **Editorial responsibility** (138): ultimate legal responsibility for the publication; the identity and contact of the responsible person or company should be publicly available in an easily findable place (e.g. footer, terms).
- Meets it: an article written by a human and translated by AI, after human verification. Does not meet it: articles checked by another AI plus a cursory grammar check by a human.
- The Code (Commitment 4) adds for entities outside the media: an internal policy naming the person with editorial responsibility (name, role, contact) and a description of organizational measures, without documenting every publication.

## Form and moment of disclosure

- At the latest at the recipient's first contact with the content, for every copy and every person (141).
- A disclosure that is easy to overlook is not "clear and distinguishable", e.g. hidden in terms or a menu tab.
- Disclosure only at the start of a video is not enough for someone who did not watch from the beginning: repeat it during the video.
- (117) The deployer **may not rely on invisible machine marking** (metadata, C2PA, watermark) from the tool provider: the disclosure is to be visible or audible to a human without tools.
- (154) Content generated before 2.08.2026 and already published does not need a retroactive label; generated earlier but published from 2.08.2026, it does.
